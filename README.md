## Case 1: Skills Development Service for IT Specialists

**Context**  
A corporate L&D platform serving 14,000+ IT and Digital specialists. The existing learning tools had low adoption and NPS at 32.

**Problem**  
Specialists were not engaging with available learning content. The catalog was generic, navigation was unclear, and there was no link between learning and career growth.

**Hypothesis**  
Personalized development pathways and a clearer onboarding flow would increase engagement and satisfaction.

**Solution**
- Launched a new skills development service from scratch with a team of 15.
- Conducted 20+ in-depth user interviews to identify pain points.
- Built a prioritized backlog focused on personalization and quick wins.
- Designed role- and grade-based development tracks.

**Metrics**
- 25% adoption within 4 months.
- NPS: 32 → 65 (doubled).
- 1,500+ employees entered the talent pool.
- Voluntary turnover reduced by 8% in pilot units.

**Learnings**  
Personalization matters more than content volume. Users need to see *why* a specific course matters for their career, not just *what* is available.

## Case 2: AI Pilots in Corporate Learning

**Context**  
A corporate university with 90,000+ employees. The R&D function was exploring AI adoption across learning products.

**Problem**  
Operational errors were recurring, and correction was slow. Traditional training was not reinforcing the right behaviors at the right moment.

**Hypothesis**  
Voice simulation and trigger-based learning could improve retention and reduce operational errors.

**Solution**
- Piloted voice simulation for hands-on practice.
- Introduced trigger-based learning (short modules delivered at the moment of need).
- Measured impact over 6 months.

**Metrics**
- Operational errors: −6%.
- On-time error correction: +12%.

**Learnings**  
AI works best when it augments existing workflows, not when it replaces them. Trigger-based delivery was more effective than standalone modules.

## Case 3: Event-Based Chatbot for Conversion

**Context**  
An entrepreneurship education platform with 300,000+ graduates. Events were driving registrations but conversion to banking products was low.

**Problem**  
Participant communication was manual and inconsistent. Follow-ups were delayed, and there was no automated way to move users from event to product.

**Hypothesis**  
An event-based chatbot could automate communication and increase conversion.

**Solution**
- Built and launched a chatbot that automated participant communication.
- Designed triggers based on user behavior during and after events.
- Ran A/B tests to optimize messaging.

**Metrics**
- Conversion from events to flagship banking product: +70%.
- Email segment grew 30% MoM.

**Learnings**  
Automation works when it feels personal. The chatbot succeeded because it was timely and relevant, not because it was automated.

## Case 4: Referral Program — A Failed Bet

**Context**  
An early-stage EdTech startup with a platform-based product. The team was looking for additional monetization streams beyond the core offering.

**Problem**  
Growth was slowing, and acquisition costs were rising. The founder believed that a loyal, engaged community could become ambassadors and bring in new users through referrals.

**Hypothesis**  
If we turn our most engaged users into ambassadors, they will bring friends and colleagues, driving traffic and additional sales.

**Solution (as built)**
- Launched a referral program with a full ambassador infrastructure: training, events, networking, a personal ambassador dashboard, referral links and generation.
- Participation was free; ambassadors were paid a share of purchases made through their referrals.
- The entire infrastructure (training, platform, links, dashboard) was built by my team and me.

**What went wrong**  
We invested heavily in infrastructure before validating demand. We never asked users whether they actually wanted this. We built a product that seemed exciting to us but was not solving a real pain point for the audience. The hypothesis was pitched by the founder and accepted without sufficient discovery.

**Metrics**
- 25% of email traffic registered (target was 10%, above expectations).
- Engagement and completed transactions were far below target.
- No meaningful traffic or revenue impact after 6 months.

**Outcome**  
The program was frozen after 6 months. A painful and expensive lesson.

**Learnings**  
Validate demand before building complex infrastructure. Even a trusted, authoritative opinion does not replace data. Now I always start with interviews, small tests, and real user questions before scaling anything.

## Case 5: Gamification That Backfired — How We Fixed It

**Context**  
A corporate learning platform for 10,000+ employees, part of a large enterprise. One of the strategic priorities was increasing employee engagement with internal development tools: learning, knowledge sharing, assessments, and talent pool participation.

**Problem**  
Engagement with learning content was low. completion rate was underperforming against targets.

**Hypothesis**  
Gamification (points for completed lessons, levels, visual achievements) would increase engagement and completion.

**Solution (first iteration)**
- Added points for completed lessons, levels, and achievement badges.
- The mechanic was migrated from other business segments where it had worked well.

**What happened**  
COR went up. Everyone was satisfied (until I looked deeper).
- Many lessons were being completed in 1–2 minutes, while the content was designed for 10–15 minutes.
- Segmentation showed that the users with the sharpest increase in speed were those chasing ranking and badges (which at the time gave nothing beyond a mark in their profile).
- In-depth interviews with "active" users revealed they were scrolling through content without reading, just to earn points and climb the leaderboard.
- In courses with built-in knowledge checks, correct-answer rates dropped module by module among the "fast" users.

We had built a mechanic that incentivized simulated activity instead of actual learning. The pattern of content consumption and motivation in the IT segment differed from other segments where the mechanic had originally worked.

**Solution (second iteration)**
- Removed all achievements for the mere act of completing content.
- Shifted the focus from speed and volume to quality: achievements for mastering a new technology with a condition of implementing a real project using it; achievements for mentoring and coaching less experienced colleagues.
- Added thresholds so that simply scrolling through a course became impossible.
- Launched an A/B test: old gamification vs. new.

**Metrics**
- Completed lessons decreased in the pilot group.
- Time per session increased.
- Day-30 retention grew by 30%.
- Replaced most quality metrics with one core metric, and added a team review metric to measure transformation rate (TR) after training and real changes in work processes.

**Outcome**  
We returned meaning to the product. People started using the platform and development tools, and, more importantly, applying the practices in their work, not just clicking for a badge.

**Learnings**  
Metrics can lie if you don't look beneath the surface. Gamification must match the motivation patterns of the specific audience. And when a mechanic "migrates" from another segment, it must be re-validated, not copied.
